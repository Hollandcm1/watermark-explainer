The pattern the detector actually sees

Scored under Key A: each point is the g-value of the word that's actually in that slot. The detector never sees a "base" sentence — it just measures how high each chosen word leans toward the key. Text written with Key A rides near the top; everything else jitters around chance (0.5). Summing that elevation is the whole test.

Why only the six slots? The grey skeleton words ("The", "were", "and"…) are near-forced — one word is the obvious choice, so the watermark has nothing to swap and can't leave a mark there. All of the signal lives in the blue slots, where several words were plausible and the key got to pick. And it's statistical, not a fingerprint: one high point means nothing, but six words all riding high under the same key is a pattern unmarked text won't produce. Fewer steerable slots (short or formulaic text) = weaker, harder-to-detect watermark.

The takeaway

Each swap is invisible in isolation — every version reads naturally and means the same thing. But across the six slots, Key A's choices consistently score high on Key A's g-values (and Key B's on Key B's). A detector holding the key sums those scores and sees a pattern no unmarked text would produce. Copy-paste preserves it; paraphrasing rewrites the tokens and erases it.
